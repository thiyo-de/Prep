## 1. 🧱 Start here: the pieces of a tense

### 1.1 Meaning before memorisation

Think of a video. You can describe a person's **routine**, pause on an **action in progress**, count **completed tasks**, or measure **how long an activity has continued**. These are different ways of viewing an action.

| Term | Simple meaning | தமிழில் |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Who or what the sentence is about: **Ravi** works | எழுவாய்; யாரைப் பற்றிச் சொல்கிறோம்? |
| Verb | Action or state: work, know, be | வினைச்சொல்; செயல் அல்லது நிலை |
| Main verb | Carries the main meaning: has **written** | முக்கிய வினைச்சொல் |
| Auxiliary / helping verb | Helps build the form: **has** written | துணை வினைச்சொல் |
| Tense | Grammatical form connected with time | காலத்துடன் தொடர்புடைய வினை வடிவம் |
| Aspect | How an event is viewed: ongoing, completed, etc. | செயல் நடைபெறும் விதம் அல்லது நிலை |
| Reference point | The time from which we look at an event | ஒப்பிட்டுப் பார்க்கும் நேரம் |
| Duration | Length of time: two hours | கால அளவு |
| Clause | A unit containing a subject and a verb | எழுவாயும் வினையும் உள்ள பகுதி |
| Stative verb | Expresses a state: know, own | நிலையை உணர்த்தும் வினைச்சொல் |
| Dynamic verb | Expresses an activity: run, write | செயலை உணர்த்தும் வினைச்சொல் |

The PDF uses the familiar **12 tense forms**: three time groups × four forms. Use that system for learning. More precisely, it combines tense, aspect and future constructions; English does not have 12 separate verb endings. **Perfect** does not mean “excellent”; it looks back from a reference point. It does not always mean the situation has stopped: “I have known her for years” can still be true now.

### 1.2 V1–V5: decode the PDF's labels

| Label | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | Base form | go, write, work, have |
| V2 | Past form | went, wrote, worked, had |
| V3 | Past participle; used after perfect **have** | gone, written, worked, had |
| V4 | The **-ing** form | going, writing, working, having |
| V5 | Present **-s/-es** form | goes, writes, works, has |

**V4/V5 are classroom labels**, not extra tenses. A participle is a verb form used in a larger structure. “Written” alone does not tell you whether the whole sentence is present perfect, past perfect or passive.

**Agreement basics:** I/you/we/they **work, have**; he/she/it **works, has**. Present **be**: I **am**, he/she/it **is**, you/we/they **are**. Past **be**: I/he/she/it **was**, you/we/they **were**. **Had** and **will** do not change with singular/plural subjects.

**Spelling reminders:** work → works; watch → watches; study → studies; play → plays. Write → writing; run → running; lie → lying. Learn irregular V2/V3 pairs separately: see–saw–seen, take–took–taken, do–did–done, come–came–come, leave–left–left, know–knew–known, read–read–read. Present *read* sounds like “reed”; past/participle *read* sounds like “red”.

### 1.3 Make negatives and questions

**Negative:** add **not after the first auxiliary**. **Question:** move that first auxiliary before the subject.

| Statement | Negative | Yes/no question |
|---|---|---|
| She works. | She **does not work**. | **Does** she **work**? |
| They work. | They **do not work**. | **Do** they **work**? |
| She worked. | She **did not work**. | **Did** she **work**? |
| She is working. | She **is not working**. | **Is** she **working**? |
| She has worked. | She **has not worked**. | **Has** she **worked**? |
| She has been working. | She **has not been working**. | **Has** she **been working**? |
| She had been working. | She **had not been working**. | **Had** she **been working**? |
| She will have worked. | She **will not have worked**. | **Will** she **have worked**? |

Simple present/past action verbs need **do/does/did** when no auxiliary is already present. After these, use **V1**: not “does works” or “did went”. With **be**, use it directly: “Is she ready?”, “They were not absent.” For a subject question, no extra *do* is normally needed: **Who wrote this?** Compare the object question **What did she write?**

Contractions: don't = do not; doesn't = does not; didn't = did not; isn't/aren't = is not/are not; wasn't/weren't = was not/were not; haven't/hasn't/hadn't = have/has/had not; won't = will not. **He's** can mean *he is* or *he has*: “He's reading” / “He's finished.” **He'd** can mean *he had* or *he would*: “He'd left” / “He'd leave.” Read the following form.

### 1.4 Five foundation models — added teaching examples

**A1. Identify the main verb and auxiliary: “She has written a letter.”**

1. Writing is the action, so **written** is the main verb, V3.
2. **Has** helps build the perfect form and agrees with *she*.

**Answer:** has = auxiliary; written = main verb. **Check:** the whole form is *has written*, not just *written*.

**A2. Correct: “Does he goes to school?”**

1. **Does** already marks the present singular question.
2. Put the main verb in V1: **go**.

**Answer:** Does he go to school? **Trap:** do not mark the same grammatical job twice.

**A3. Identify: “They were absent.”**

1. **Absent** describes a state; it is an adjective, not an -ing verb.
2. **Were** is the past form of *be*.

**Answer:** simple past of *be*. **Check:** *was/were* alone does not prove past continuous.

**A4. Turn “She will have finished” into a negative question.**

1. The first auxiliary is **will**; move it before *she*.
2. Place **not** after the subject in the full question form; leave *have finished* unchanged.

**Answer:** Will she not have finished? **Check:** also “Won't she have finished?”; not “Will she has finished?”

**A5. Are “I had lunch” and “I had had lunch” the same tense?**

1. In the first, **had** alone is the main verb, V2.
2. In the second, first **had** is the auxiliary; second **had** is V3 of *have*.

**Answer:** simple past / past perfect. **Check:** repeating *had* can be necessary and correct.

## 2. 🗺️ The twelve-form map

Here **S** means subject. Formulas show statements in the **active voice**; passive forms need a separate voice lesson. Use the negative/question method above for every row.

### Present: look from now

| Form | Statement pattern | Main viewpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present / present indefinite | S + V1/V5 | Habit, fact, state |
| Present continuous / progressive | S + am/is/are + V4 | Activity in progress or temporary situation |
| Present perfect | S + have/has + V3 | Past connected with now |
| Present perfect continuous | S + have/has + been + V4 | Activity/duration leading up to now |

### Past: look from a past point

| Form | Statement pattern | Main viewpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Simple past / past indefinite | S + V2 | Past event, habit or state |
| Past continuous / progressive | S + was/were + V4 | Activity in progress then |
| Past perfect | S + had + V3 | Earlier than a past reference point |
| Past perfect continuous | S + had + been + V4 | Activity/duration leading up to a past point |

### Future: look towards/from a future point

| Form | Statement pattern | Main viewpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Simple future / future indefinite | S + will + V1 | Prediction, decision, promise |
| Future continuous | S + will + be + V4 | Activity in progress then |
| Future perfect | S + will + have + V3 | Completed by a future reference point |
| Future perfect continuous | S + will + have + been + V4 | Duration reaching a future reference point |

The source also uses **imperfect** for continuous and **shall** alongside *will*. In ordinary future statements, **will works with all subjects**. *Shall* with *I/we* is more formal; **Shall I…? / Shall we…?** often offers help or makes a suggestion. Do not learn “I will” as an error. [Cambridge: will and shall](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/future-will-and-shall).

> **🔬 Process map: choose a form**
>
> 1. Find the reference point: **now / past / future**.
> 2. Decide the meaning: **routine/state / in progress / looking back / duration**.
> 3. Check whether the verb expresses an action or a state.
> 4. Build the auxiliary chain and agree it with the subject.
> 5. Check the whole sentence, especially time and condition clauses.

## 3. 🟢 Present forms: understand, then apply

### 3.1 Simple present — வழக்கம் / பொதுவான உண்மை / நிலை

**Concept:** describes habits, facts and states. “I study at night” gives my routine; it does not say I am studying at this exact second. It also serves timetables and ordinary future time/condition clauses.

**Formula:** I/you/we/they + V1; he/she/it + V5. With *be*, use am/is/are.

**Clues:** usually, often, every day, general facts. These help, but do not decide the tense alone. A past habit can also contain *every day*.

> **தமிழில்:** “நான் தினமும் படிக்கிறேன்” என்பது பழக்கம். “நான் இப்போது படித்துக்கொண்டிருக்கிறேன்” என்பது இப்போது நடைபெறும் செயல். இரண்டுக்கும் ஒரே ஆங்கில வடிவம் இல்லை.

**B1. Fill: “He ___ his work on time.” Use *complete* for his regular habit.**

1. Regular habit → simple present.
2. *He* is singular → **completes**.

**Answer:** He completes his work on time. **Check:** *complete* alone does not agree with *he*. Adapted from p.7.

**B2. Make negative: “I have my lunch at 2 pm.”**

1. *Have* means eat; it is the main verb here.
2. Use **do not + have** for a routine negative.

**Answer:** I do not have my lunch at 2 pm. **Trap:** *have* does not automatically mean present perfect. Source sentence: p.7.

**B3. “The match ___ at 2 pm tomorrow.” Use the official timetable: begin.**

1. A timetable can use simple present for a future event.
2. *The match* takes V5.

**Answer:** begins. **Check:** future time and future grammatical form are different ideas. Adapted from p.18.

**B4. Correct both errors: “She don't studies on Sundays.”**

1. Singular *she* requires **doesn't**.
2. After *doesn't*, use **study**, not *studies*.

**Answer:** She doesn't study on Sundays. **Check:** subject agreement belongs on the auxiliary here.

**B5. Must “I brushed my teeth every day” become “I brush…”?**

1. *Every day* tells frequency, not whether the habit is past or present.
2. “When I lived there, I brushed my teeth every day” is a valid past habit.

**Answer:** no; choose *brush* only for a present habit. **Trap:** the PDF's p.17 correction needs that intended context.

### 3.2 Present continuous — இப்போது / தற்காலிகமாக நடைபெறும் செயல்

**Concept:** am/is/are + V4 shows activity in progress around now, a temporary situation, or a future arrangement. It need not be happening at the exact instant of speaking: “I am reading a long book this week.”

**Future arrangement:** a plan with some arrangement already made, such as a booked journey. Present continuous is not simply the “informal version” of simple present; meaning matters.

**Boredom-proof contrast:** “He teaches Physics” = usual work. “He is teaching Biology this month” = temporary assignment. **Nowadays** alone does not force continuous.

**C1. Identify: “He is writing the essay.”**

1. *Is* agrees with *he*.
2. *Writing* presents the activity in progress.

**Answer:** present continuous. **Check:** *He writing* is missing the finite helping verb. Source: p.7.

**C2. Ask a question: “The students are doing the work.”**

1. The first auxiliary is *are*.
2. Move it before the subject; keep *doing*.

**Answer:** Are the students doing the work? **Trap:** do not add *do*. Source: p.7.

**C3. Explain: “He is leaving for the meeting next week.”**

1. *Next week* supplies future time.
2. *Is leaving* presents an arranged departure.

**Answer:** present continuous used for a future arrangement. **Check:** naming the form and describing its time are separate. Source: p.22.

**C4. Correct for possession: “I am having two books.”**

1. *Have* means own/possess, a state.
2. In standard usage, choose simple present for this meaning.

**Answer:** I have two books. **Check:** “I am having tea” is different: drinking is an activity. Source: p.24.

**C5. Is “These days news travels faster” wrong?**

1. It can describe a general characteristic of the present period.
2. *Is travelling* instead highlights an ongoing development or current movement of news.

**Answer:** the simple present is valid. **Trap:** do not mechanically apply the PDF's p.15 change to every sentence with *these days*.

### 3.3 Present perfect — கடந்த காலத்திற்கும் இப்போதுக்கும் தொடர்பு

**Formula:** have/has + V3. Use it for experience up to now, a completed action with present relevance, or a situation continuing to now.

Compare “I **lost** my key yesterday” (a specified past event) with “I **have lost** my key” (the loss matters now). For a completed event at a specified, finished past time, use simple past, not present perfect. *Today/this week* can be viewed as open or as containing a finished occasion. [Cambridge: past simple or present perfect](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/uk/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-simple).

**Since** identifies the starting point: since 2016, since morning, since I joined. **For** gives duration: for two hours, for a long time. Neither word means “always use continuous”.

**D1. Fill: “The students ___ arrived for the meeting.”**

1. *Arrived* is V3 in this perfect construction.
2. Plural *students* takes **have**.

**Answer:** have arrived. **Check:** *has* would not agree. Source: p.8.

**D2. Correct: “I have done the work yesterday.”**

1. *Yesterday* places this completed work in a finished past period.
2. Use V2 **did** and remove *have*.

**Answer:** I did the work yesterday. **Check:** “I have done the work” is valid without that finished-time claim. Source: p.34.

**D3. Complete for a continuing relationship: “I ___ him for a long time.” (know)**

1. The state extends from the past to now.
2. *Know* normally takes the simple perfect, not continuous.

**Answer:** have known. **Trap:** not *have been knowing*. Source: pp.31–32.

**D4. Explain: “He has had his lunch.”**

1. First *has* is the present perfect auxiliary.
2. *Had* is V3 of main verb *have*, meaning eat here.

**Answer:** present perfect. **Check:** “He has has lunch” has the wrong participle. Source: pp.14,58–60.

**D5. Choose for a completed count: “The player ___ ten centuries since 2016.”**

1. The focus is **ten completed centuries**, not time spent scoring.
2. Use present perfect simple to total the achievements.

**Answer:** has scored. **Check:** “has been scoring centuries” can emphasise repeated activity, without that completed total. Source: p.40.

### 3.4 Present perfect continuous — கடந்த காலத்திலிருந்து இப்போது வரை செயல்

**Formula:** have/has + been + V4. Focus on an activity's continuation or duration up to now. It may still be continuing, or may have just stopped with visible results: “I am tired because I have been running.” **Since/for is optional**, not part of the compulsory verb chain.

**Timeline:** start in the past → activity → **NOW**. Tamil: “காலை முதல் படித்துக்கொண்டிருக்கிறேன்.”

**E1. Fill: “I ___ reading since morning.”**

1. The activity started earlier and reaches now.
2. *I* takes *have*; continuous perfect needs *been*.

**Answer:** have been reading. **Check:** not *have reading*. Source: p.8.

**E2. Choose since/for: “He has been working ___ two months.”**

1. Two months is a duration, not a starting date.
2. Duration takes **for**.

**Answer:** for. **Check:** *since January* would identify a starting point. Source: p.8.

**E3. “She has been working.” Is a duration phrase missing?**

1. *Has + been + working* is already a complete verb phrase.
2. Context may explain the duration or focus on recent activity.

**Answer:** the sentence is grammatical. **Trap:** since/for need not appear. Source: p.14.

**E4. Repair for an ongoing residence: “He is living in this town for the last two years.”**

1. The stated period began before now and continues now.
2. Choose **has been living** to emphasise continuation.

**Answer:** He has been living in this town for the last two years. **Check:** *has lived* is also possible; *live* permits both. Source: p.38.

**E5. “I have been reading for four hours.” Have I finished the book?**

1. The sentence measures reading activity.
2. It does not state that the entire book is completed.

**Answer:** completion is unknown. **Check:** compare “I have read four chapters” for completed quantity. Source duration example: p.12.

## 4. 🟠 Past forms: separate the event from the background

### 4.1 Simple past — கடந்த நிகழ்வு / பழக்கம் / நிலை

**Formula:** S + V2; negative did not + V1; question Did + S + V1? With *be*, use was/were directly.

It describes a past event or state, not only actions with a written time word. “He went” is grammatical if the past occasion is understood. It also describes past habits. **Used to + V1** describes a former habit/state; **would + V1** can describe repeated past actions when the past setting is established. “I used to own a bicycle” describes a state; ordinary habitual *would own* does not express that same meaning.

**F1. Identify: “He visited the zoo yesterday.”**

1. The visit belongs to yesterday.
2. *Visited* is V2 here.

**Answer:** simple past. **Check:** no *has* before it for this finished occasion. Source: p.9.

**F2. Make negative: “They were absent from the class.”**

1. *Were* is the past form of *be*.
2. Add *not* after it.

**Answer:** They were not absent from the class. **Trap:** not “did not were”. Source: p.9.

**F3. Correct: “Did she saw the film?”**

1. *Did* already marks the past question.
2. Use V1 **see**, not V2 *saw*.

**Answer:** Did she see the film? **Check:** the statement is “She saw the film.”

**F4. Explain: “He used to study late hours.”**

1. *Used to* places a repeated activity in an earlier period.
2. *Study* stays V1 after *used to*.

**Answer:** a former study habit; natural wording also includes “study until late”. **Check:** *be used to studying* instead means be accustomed to studying. Source: p.47; contrast added.

**F5. Must “She had seen the movie yesterday” always be wrong?**

1. As an isolated past report, **saw** is the natural answer.
2. With a later past viewpoint, past perfect can fit: “She told me last night that she had seen the movie yesterday morning.”

**Answer:** context decides; *yesterday* alone does not ban past perfect. **Trap:** p.42's simple-past correction assumes a standalone report.

### 4.2 Past continuous — அந்த நேரத்தில் நடந்துகொண்டிருந்த செயல்

**Formula:** was/were + V4. It supplies a past scene/background or activity in progress. A shorter event may happen during it: “I was reading when the lights went out.” *While* often introduces the longer activity, but is not an automatic continuous-tense command.

**G1. Identify: “The teacher was conducting the exam.”**

1. *Was* fixes a past viewpoint.
2. *Conducting* shows an activity in progress.

**Answer:** past continuous. **Check:** conducting = நடத்திக்கொண்டிருத்தல். Source: p.9.

**G2. Make a question: “They were not responding to the question.”**

1. Move *were* before the subject.
2. Keep *not responding* after the subject.

**Answer:** Were they not responding to the question? **Check:** “Weren't they responding…?” is the contraction. Source: p.9.

**G3. Fill: “I sprained my ankle while I ___.” (play)**

1. The ankle injury is the shorter event.
2. The background activity was already in progress.

**Answer:** was playing. **Check:** sprained = சுளுக்கிக்கொண்டேன். Source: p.50.

**G4. Choose for an existing queue: “When the film began, we stood / were standing in the queue.”**

1. We were already in the queue when the film started.
2. Use continuous for that background situation.

**Answer:** were standing. **Check:** *stood* could describe taking a position then, changing the story. Source: p.52.

**G5. Can both “I played yesterday” and “I was playing yesterday” be correct?**

1. *Played* presents the past activity as a whole.
2. *Was playing* views it as ongoing during some past interval.

**Answer:** yes. **Trap:** *yesterday* distinguishes past from now; it does not alone choose the aspect. Source: p.52.

### 4.3 Past perfect — ஒரு கடந்த நேரத்திற்கு முன்

**Formula:** had + V3. Look backwards from a past reference point. “The train **had left** when I **arrived**” means its departure was already complete at my arrival.

**Timeline:** earlier departure → later arrival → now.

**தமிழில்:** நான் சென்றடைவதற்கு முன்பே ரயில் புறப்பட்டுவிட்டது. எந்த நிகழ்வு முதலில் நடந்தது என்பதைத் தெளிவாக்குகிறது.

Do not apply “two past verbs = compulsory past perfect”. *Before/after* can already establish order, and simple past may be sufficient. **When** + simple past can mean events occurred together/in sequence; **when** + past perfect highlights prior completion. [Cambridge: before](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/before), [Cambridge: past perfect or past simple](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/past-perfect-simple).

**H1. Explain: “They had left before I came.”**

1. Their leaving is earlier than my arrival.
2. *Had left* makes that earlier completion explicit.

**Answer:** had left = past perfect; came = simple past. **Check:** left is V3 as well as V2; its helper tells you its role. Source: p.10.

**H2. Choose for all films viewed up to that past time: “That was the worst film that I saw / had seen.”**

1. *Was* gives the past comparison point.
2. Earlier viewing experience is measured up to that point.

**Answer:** had seen. **Check:** without “up to that time”, a different context could support *saw*. Source: p.54.

**H3. Explain: “I had had my lunch before the meeting began.”**

1. First *had* builds past perfect.
2. Second *had* is V3 of the main verb *have*.

**Answer:** lunch was already eaten before the meeting. **Check:** removing either *had* changes the verb structure. Source core: p.59; time clause added.

**H4. Is “The thief escaped before I opened the door” ungrammatical?**

1. *Before* already gives the order.
2. *Had escaped* adds emphasis on prior completion, but simple past can also work.

**Answer:** the original is grammatical. **Trap:** p.53's correction to *had escaped* is a teaching preference, not a universal necessity.

**H5. Compare “When I arrived, the train left” and “…the train had left.”**

1. *Left* presents departure at/about the arrival time.
2. *Had left* puts departure before arrival; I was too late for that train.

**Answer:** both possible, different meanings. **Check:** do not “correct” the story without checking the intended sequence.

### 4.4 Past perfect continuous — ஒரு கடந்த நேரம் வரை தொடர்ந்த செயல்

**Formula:** had + been + V4. An activity continues towards a past point, or has recently stopped and explains a past result. “I was tired because I had been running.” The past reference can come from an earlier sentence.

**I1. Explain: “They had been working for a week.”**

1. *Had been working* looks back from a past viewpoint.
2. *For a week* measures the activity's duration.

**Answer:** past perfect continuous. **Check:** a fuller context could be “when the supervisor arrived”. Source: p.10.

**I2. Choose since/for: “He had been sleeping ___ afternoon.”**

1. Afternoon is the starting point, not a measured length.
2. Use **since**.

**Answer:** since afternoon. **Check:** *for three hours* instead measures duration. Source: p.10.

**I3. Repair: “I was waiting for you since morning, but you did not turn up on time.”**

1. The waiting began before the past deadline/expected arrival.
2. Show duration up to that past point with *had been waiting*.

**Answer:** I had been waiting for you since morning, but you did not turn up on time. **Check:** *turn up* means arrive. Source: p.62.

**I4. Make a question: “She had been studying for two hours when I called.”**

1. The first auxiliary is *had*.
2. Move it before *she*, preserving *been studying*.

**Answer:** Had she been studying for two hours when I called? **Check:** not “Had she was studying?”

**I5. Fill: “By 2020, I ___ him for ten years.” (know)**

1. The state reaches a past reference point, 2020.
2. *Know* normally takes simple perfect, not continuous.

**Answer:** had known. **Trap:** a long duration does not force *had been knowing*.

## 5. 🔵 Future forms: prediction, progress, completion, duration

### 5.1 Simple future and other future expressions — எதிர்காலம்

**Will + V1:** a prediction, promise, offer or decision. **Be going to + V1:** an intention/plan or a prediction based on present evidence. **Be about to + V1:** something is very close to happening. *Going to* is **not restricted to the near future**.

**Compare:** “I will help” (offer); “I am going to revise tonight” (plan); “The train leaves at six” (timetable); “I am meeting my teacher tomorrow” (arrangement). These meanings can overlap; context and task instructions determine the best answer. [Cambridge: going to](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/going-to.), [Cambridge: future forms and their limits](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/grammar-for-english-language-teachers/future/42F99CBD7C7C9414B3FA20999CA01457).

**J1. Identify: “I will do the work on time.”**

1. *Will* is followed by base form *do*.
2. Here it expresses a future commitment.

**Answer:** simple future. **Check:** not “will does/did”. Source: p.63.

**J2. Is “I shall return by Monday” correct?**

1. *Shall* can be used with *I* for a formal future statement.
2. *By Monday* gives a deadline: no later than Monday.

**Answer:** yes; “I will return by Monday” is also correct. **Trap:** *by* does not automatically require future perfect. Source: p.63.

**J3. Explain: “His father is about to retire.”**

1. *Be about to + V1* shows an imminent event: very soon.
2. *Retire* means stop regular employment, usually at the end of a career.

**Answer:** retirement is near. **Check:** not “is about to retiring”. Source: p.64.

**J4. Identify the full structure: “The train is going to enter the platform.”**

1. *Is going to* is followed by base verb *enter*.
2. It predicts a future event; this is not *is entering*.

**Answer:** a *going-to* future construction. **Check:** “is going to the station” instead can describe physical movement. Source: p.22.

**J5. Is “I hope to finish the work soon” simple future in form?**

1. The finite verb is **hope**, simple present.
2. The intended finishing lies in the future, but *to finish* is an infinitive.

**Answer:** future meaning expressed through a present-tense sentence. **Trap:** do not label every future meaning *will + V1*. Source: p.63.

### 5.2 Future continuous — எதிர்காலத்தில் ஒரு நேரத்தில் நடைபெறும் செயல்

**Formula:** will + be + V4. Imagine pausing a video at a future time: the activity is in progress then. It can also describe expected future activity. [British Council: future continuous and future perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/future-continuous-future-perfect?page=1).

**K1. Complete: “At 9 tonight, I ___ the meeting.” (attend; show activity in progress)**

1. The reference point is 9 tonight.
2. In-progress future → *will be + attending*.

**Answer:** will be attending. **Check:** *will attending* omits *be*. Adapted from p.11.

**K2. Make negative: “He will be watching the movie.”**

1. First auxiliary = *will*.
2. Add *not* immediately after it.

**Answer:** He will not be watching the movie. **Check:** keep *be watching*. Source: p.65.

**K3. Explain: “He will be working there till Monday.”**

1. The work continues through an interval towards Monday.
2. *Till* means until, the endpoint of continuation.

**Answer:** future continuous. **Check:** *till* is not the same as deadline marker *by*. Source: p.65.

**K4. Ask about the expected visit: “The postman will be coming soon.”**

1. Move the first auxiliary *will*.
2. Retain the subject followed by *be coming*.

**Answer:** Will the postman be coming soon? **Check:** no *does*. Source: p.65.

**K5. Can “I will be knowing the result tomorrow” express ordinary knowledge naturally?**

1. *Know* expresses the state of having the information.
2. Use simple *will know*, not continuous for that ordinary meaning.

**Answer:** I will know the result tomorrow. **Trap:** future time does not remove the stative-verb distinction.

### 5.3 Future perfect — எதிர்காலக் குறிப்பிட்ட நேரத்திற்குள் முடிந்திருக்கும்

**Formula:** will + have + V3. Look back from a future deadline/reference point. “By Friday, I will have finished” presents the finishing as already complete when Friday is reached.

**Timeline:** now → completion → **future checkpoint**. *By* means no later than; *before* means earlier than. Neither word makes one tense compulsory regardless of meaning.

**L1. Choose to emphasise completed reading: “He will read / will have read the books by the end of the vacation.”**

1. The viewpoint is the end of the vacation.
2. We are asked to show the books already read by then.

**Answer:** will have read. **Check:** *will read … by…* can also promise completion; the emphasis differs. Source: p.66.

**L2. Correct: “By this time next year, he has had settled in London with his parents.”**

1. The reference point is future, not present/past.
2. Replace *has had settled* with **will have settled**.

**Answer:** By this time next year, he will have settled in London with his parents. **Check:** after *will*, use *have*, not *has*. Source: p.66.

**L3. Show earlier arrival: “The train ___ before we reach the station.” (arrive)**

1. Our reaching is the future reference point.
2. Earlier completed arrival → *will have arrived*.

**Answer:** The train will have arrived before we reach the station. **Check:** *reach*, not ordinary future *will reach*, in the time clause. Source: p.66.

**L4. Turn “She will have completed the course by June” into a question.**

1. Move the first auxiliary *will* before *she*.
2. Keep *have completed* together.

**Answer:** Will she have completed the course by June? **Check:** not “Will she have complete…?”

**L5. Does every sentence with “by Monday” require future perfect?**

1. A deadline can occur with a simple promise: “I will return by Monday.”
2. Future perfect instead looks back from that deadline: “By Monday, I will have returned.”

**Answer:** no. **Trap:** *by* is a meaning clue, not a tense selector on its own.

### 5.4 Future perfect continuous — எதிர்கால நேரம் வரை செயல் நீடித்திருக்கும்

**Formula:** will + have + been + V4. Measures how long an activity will have continued when a future point is reached. The activity may begin before now or later. The future reference can be understood from context; supplying it helps beginners.

**M1. Add a future viewpoint to “He will have been teaching here for two years.”**

1. *For two years* is duration.
2. Add **by next June** to make the future measuring point explicit.

**Answer:** By next June, he will have been teaching here for two years. **Check:** the added date is illustrative, not in the PDF. Source: p.67.

**M2. Identify: “I shall have been living in this town for a year.”**

1. *Shall + have + been + living* is the full auxiliary chain.
2. The duration is measured at a future point understood from context.

**Answer:** future perfect continuous. **Check:** “will have been living” is also valid. Source: p.67.

**M3. Make negative: “By noon, they will have been waiting for two hours.”**

1. First auxiliary = *will*.
2. Place *not* after it; preserve the remaining chain.

**Answer:** By noon, they will not have been waiting for two hours. **Check:** this denies the two-hour duration; it need not mean no waiting at all.

**M4. Choose for duration: “By 8 pm, I ___ for three hours.” (study; focus on sustained activity)**

1. Future measuring point = 8 pm.
2. Duration of activity → *will have been studying*.

**Answer:** will have been studying. **Check:** *will have studied three chapters* would count completed output instead.

**M5. Correct: “By May, I will have been knowing him for a year.”**

1. *Know* describes a continuing state.
2. Use the simple perfect form with this state.

**Answer:** By May, I will have known him for a year. **Trap:** duration does not make a stative verb continuous.

## 6. 🧠 State or action? The verb's meaning decides

The PDF calls these **static verbs**; *stative verbs* is the usual term. For ordinary states, prefer simple forms: **know, believe, belong, own, seem, understand**. However, a verb may have both a state meaning and an activity meaning. Learn the meaning in the sentence, not a permanent ban on its spelling. [British Council: stative verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/stative-verbs).

| Verb | State meaning | Activity meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Have | I **have** a book — possession | I **am having** lunch — eating |
| See | I **see** the hill — perception | I **am seeing** the principal tomorrow — meeting |
| Hear | I **can hear** music — perception | The judge **is hearing** the case — conducting proceedings |
| Appear | It **appears** difficult — seems | The actor **is appearing** on stage — performing |
| Smell | The flower **smells** sweet — quality | She **is smelling** the flower — deliberately sniffing |
| Think | I **think** it is correct — opinion | I **am thinking** about the answer — mental activity |
| Remember | I **remember** his name — stored knowledge | I **am remembering** more details — active recollection |

> **தமிழில்:** “அவனிடம் புத்தகம் உள்ளது” என்பது நிலை. “அவன் மதிய உணவு சாப்பிட்டுக்கொண்டிருக்கிறான்” என்பது செயல். இரண்டிலும் *have* வரலாம்; பொருள் மாறுவதால் tense வடிவமும் மாறலாம்.

**Important correction:** “I am hoping for the best” is valid. Continuous forms can present some mental states as current processes. The PDF's blanket rejection on p.30 should not be memorised. [Cambridge University Press: mental-state verbs and continuous forms](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811074/81114/excerpt/9781107481114_excerpt.pdf).

### Five meaning-check models

**N1. Correct: “I am knowing the truth.”**

1. *Know* describes possession of knowledge here.
2. Use simple present for this ordinary state.

**Answer:** I know the truth. **Check:** not *am know*. Source: p.23.

**N2. Is “She is having tea” a possession error?**

1. *Having tea* means drinking tea.
2. Drinking is an activity in progress.

**Answer:** correct. **Check:** “She has a teapot” describes possession instead. Source: p.24.

**N3. Explain the two forms in “Seeing is believing.”**

1. *Seeing* and *believing* function as noun-like expressions for activities/ideas.
2. The finite linking verb is **is**; the sentence is not describing somebody currently believing something.

**Answer:** these -ing forms are traditionally called gerunds. **Trap:** an -ing word alone does not establish continuous tense. Source: pp.28,30.

**N4. Compare “I am seeing the movie” and “I am watching the movie.”**

1. For looking at the screen right now, **watching** is the usual choice.
2. *Seeing a movie* is also valid for attending a film, especially an arrangement: “I am seeing the movie tonight.”

**Answer:** choose according to the intended activity. **Check:** the PDF's cross is not a ban on every use of *seeing a movie*. Source: pp.28,30.

**N5. Correct only if needed: “I am hoping for the best.”**

1. *Hope* can occur in the continuous to present a current attitude or process.
2. The auxiliary and -ing form are correctly built.

**Answer:** no correction needed; “I hope for the best” is also possible. **Trap:** do not replace a valid sentence merely because of a rigid source list. Source: p.30.

## 7. 🔗 Time clauses and conditions

### 7.1 Future meaning inside a present-form clause

**Main clause:** the part that can stand independently. **Dependent clause:** a part introduced by a word such as *if* or *when* that depends on the main part.

“**I will help him** [main clause] **if he comes** [condition].” The coming is future, but an ordinary future condition uses a present form. A future time clause can use present perfect to emphasise completion: “I will leave after I **have finished**.” Present continuous can also fit a condition: “We will stay inside if it **is raining**.” [British Council: time and if clauses](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verbs-time-clauses-if-clauses).

**Source connector list:** if, when, whenever, after, before, until, unless, provided, in case, till, on the condition. Prefer the complete expressions **provided (that)** and **on condition that**.

| Connector | Meaning | Tamil reminder |
|---|---|---|
| If | A condition | என்றால் |
| Unless | Except if; often equivalent to if…not | இல்லாவிட்டால் |
| When / whenever | At the time / every time | அப்போது / எப்போதெல்லாம் |
| Before / after | Earlier / later | முன்பு / பிறகு |
| Till / until | Continuation up to a point | அந்த நேரம் வரை |
| Provided (that) / on condition that | Only on the stated condition | இந்த நிபந்தனையுடன் |
| In case | As a precaution against a possibility | ஒருவேளை நடந்தால் பயன்படுவதற்காக |

**In case is not simply if:** “Take an umbrella **in case** it rains” means take it now as a precaution. “Take an umbrella **if** it rains” makes taking it conditional on rain.

**Two limits to the no-will shortcut:** *will* can express willingness in an if-clause; and “I don't know **when he will arrive**” contains an embedded question, not an instruction about when to do something. Compare “I will call **when he arrives**.”

### Five time-clause models

**O1. Correct: “Unless you will work, you will not succeed.”**

1. *Unless* introduces the ordinary condition for future success.
2. Use present **work** in that clause; retain future in the main clause.

**Answer:** Unless you work, you will not succeed. **Check:** avoid adding a second negative inside *unless*. Source: p.20.

**O2. Correct: “Wait here till I will come.”**

1. The till-clause sets the future endpoint of waiting.
2. Use present **come** for that endpoint.

**Answer:** Wait here till I come. **Check:** the command *wait* does not need *will*. Source: p.20.

**O3. Choose: “After the letter reaches / has reached me, I shall know the result.”**

1. Both forms are allowed in a future time clause.
2. *Has reached* highlights completed arrival before knowing.

**Answer:** both are grammatical. **Trap:** p.20 marks only *reaches*, so that source item is not a reliable single-answer grammar question without further constraints.

**O4. Correct: “I will take an umbrella in case it will rains.”**

1. Ordinary precaution clause → present **rains**.
2. Independently, a modal cannot be followed by V5 *rains*.

**Answer:** I will take an umbrella in case it rains. **Check:** there are two reasons the original phrase fails. Source: p.20.

**O5. Is “Tell me when he will arrive” always wrong?**

1. It can mean “Tell me his expected arrival time.” That is an embedded question.
2. “Tell me when he arrives” can instead mean “Notify me at the moment of arrival.”

**Answer:** no; the two constructions can express different requests. **Trap:** read the job of *when*, not just the word itself.

### 7.2 Real and imagined conditions

**Hypothetical** = imagined rather than asserted as real — கற்பனையான நிலை. A past form can express distance from reality, not just distance in time.

| Type | Basic pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| General / zero | If + present, present | General result or regular relationship |
| Open future / first | If + present, will + V1 | A possible future condition and result |
| Remote present/future / second | If + past, would + V1 | An imagined or less likely situation |
| Unreal past / third | If + had + V3, would have + V3 | Imagining a different past |

**Could** and **might** can replace *would* when ability/possibility is intended. The PDF includes *should have* too; its meaning depends on context and is not freely interchangeable with *would have*. **Would go** is a modal construction, not simple past V2; **would have gone** is modal + perfect, not past perfect *had gone*. This corrects the shorthand labels on pp.56–57 and 61.

**Mixed condition, added:** “If I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now.” Past imagined cause → present imagined result. These are meaning patterns, not instructions to force every sentence into one row.

### Five conditional models

**P1. Fill an open condition: “If he comes, I ___ him.” (help)**

1. His coming is treated as a real future possibility.
2. Use *will + help* for the intended future result.

**Answer:** will help. **Check:** *comes*, not *will comes*. Source: p.61.

**P2. Explain: “If I had money, I would help the poor.”**

1. *Had* is V2 of the main verb *have*.
2. It describes an imagined present situation, followed by *would help*.

**Answer:** second conditional. **Trap:** one *had* is not automatically past perfect. Source: p.61.

**P3. Correct an unreal past condition: “If he would have come, I would have left for office.”**

1. Use **had + V3** for the imagined earlier condition.
2. Keep **would have left** for its imagined past result.

**Answer:** If he had come, I would have left for office. **Check:** this is the standard third-conditional structure. Source: p.55.

**P4. Rewrite “If he had come, I would have helped him” without if.**

1. Move **had** before the subject *he*.
2. Remove *if*; keep the result clause.

**Answer:** Had he come, I would have helped him. **Check:** this formal inversion preserves the meaning; it is not a yes/no question. Source pattern: p.55.

**P5. Explain both hads: “If we had had Mohan in our team, we would have won the match.”**

1. The first *had* is the past perfect auxiliary for an unreal past condition.
2. The second *had* means having Mohan as a team member.

**Answer:** *had had* is correct for the intended past condition. **Check:** a present-state mixed conditional is a different reading, not the target here. Source: p.61.

## 8. 🪄 Past form with present meaning: wishes and “it's time”

**It's time + subject + past form:** says something should happen now. “It's time we left” does not report that we already left. **It's time + to + V1** is a different valid pattern: “It's time to leave.” *It's high time / it's about time* with a subject and past form adds a sense that the action is overdue. [Cambridge: it's time](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/it-s-time).

**Wish + past form:** an unreal present wish. **Wish + past perfect:** regret about the past. In a formal unreal condition/wish, **were** can be used with every person: “I wish I were…”; informal *was* is also established usage. *As if/as though* can introduce a real impression or an unreal comparison; the verb form depends on that meaning. [Cambridge: wish](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/wish), [Cambridge: were and was in wishes](https://dictionaryblog.cambridge.org/2017/11/28/i-wish-id-studied-harder-expressing-regrets-and-wishes/).

> **தமிழில்:** “நான் மருத்துவராக இருந்தால் நன்றாக இருக்கும்” என்பது இப்போதைய ஆசை. ஆங்கிலத்தில் *were* வந்தாலும், கட்டாயம் கடந்த காலத்தைப் பற்றிச் சொல்லவில்லை. “நான் படித்திருந்தால் நன்றாக இருந்திருக்கும்” என்பது கடந்த கால வருத்தம்: *I wish I had studied*.

### Five special-structure models

**Q1. Choose: “It's time he goes / went / had gone to school.”**

1. The meaning is that he should go now.
2. With a subject after *it's time*, use the past form **went**.

**Answer:** went. **Check:** not an ordinary report of past travel. Source: p.43.

**Q2. Is “It's time to do the work” correct?**

1. This structure uses a to-infinitive, not a subject + finite verb clause.
2. After *to*, use V1 **do**.

**Answer:** correct. **Trap:** do not change it to “to did”. Source: p.43.

**Q3. Choose the formal unreal wish: “I wish I was / were a doctor.”**

1. The speaker imagines a different present situation.
2. Formal unreal *were* is the appropriate option for the stated register.

**Answer:** were. **Check:** the rule is about unreality, not a plural subject. Source: p.45.

**Q4. Make a past regret: “I did not read the instructions. I wish I ___ them.” (read)**

1. The regretted event is in the past.
2. Use **had + V3** after *wish*.

**Answer:** had read. **Check:** *I wish I read more* can instead describe a present habit I want to change. Added model.

**Q5. Must “He behaves as if he knows everything” always become “knew”?**

1. *Knows* can present a possible impression; *knew* distances the speaker from its truth.
2. If the intended meaning is “he does not know everything, but pretends”, choose *knew*.

**Answer:** meaning decides. **Trap:** p.45's *knew* answer fits the unreal reading; *as if* does not ban all present forms.

## 9. 🧩 Tense consistency: match the timeline, not every verb

**Reported speech** tells what someone said without directly quoting them. **Backshift** means moving a verb to a past-oriented form after a past reporting verb: says → said can accompany will → would. Backshift is often used, but is not compulsory when the information remains true/current. [British Council: reported statements](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/reported-speech-statements).

A sentence may legitimately mix past and present: “She told me that water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.” A **than** clause can compare different periods too. Do not turn “all verbs must have the same tense” into a rule.

### Five consistency models

**R1. Choose with ordinary backshift: “He said that he will / would do the work.”**

1. *Said* gives a past reporting viewpoint.
2. The future seen from that viewpoint is **would do**.

**Answer:** would. **Check:** *will* can be retained when a still-current future promise is deliberately reported that way. Source: p.47.

**R2. Explain: “The police say that he was present at the place of crime.”**

1. The police's statement is current: *say*.
2. The alleged presence belongs to an earlier time: *was*.

**Answer:** the different tenses are compatible. **Check:** present reporting does not force present event time. Source: p.48.

**R3. Compare: “He helped me more than he helps his mother” / “…helped his mother.”**

1. *Helps* compares his past help to me with his usual present help to her.
2. *Helped* compares two past amounts of help.

**Answer:** both can be grammatical. **Check:** choose the intended period, not automatic tense matching. Source: p.49.

**R4. Correct for a timeless fact: “The teacher said that the Earth revolve around the Sun.”**

1. A still-valid general fact can retain present tense after *said*.
2. Singular *Earth* requires **revolves**.

**Answer:** The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun. **Check:** agreement still matters even when tense choice is correct. Added model.

**R5. Explain: “He acts as if he had seen me before.”**

1. *Acts* describes current behaviour.
2. *Had seen* presents an earlier encounter from a remote/hypothetical viewpoint.

**Answer:** the combination is possible; it does not prove an encounter actually happened. **Trap:** do not force *acts* into past just to match *had seen*. Source: p.13.

### Set A — ten basic checks

1. For a daily habit: “Meena ___ to work by bus.” (go)
2. Correct: “Does Ravi studies at night?”

## 12. 📌 Revision card and mastery record

### The short recall card

- **Meaning first:** now/past/future + habit/progress/completion/duration.
- **Do/does/did + V1. Have/has/had + V3** when *have* is the perfect auxiliary.
- **Continuous = a form of be + V4**; an -ing word alone is not enough.
- **Since = starting point; for = duration.** Neither requires continuous automatically.
- **A finished past occasion:** simple past or another suitable past construction; avoid present perfect for that specific completed event.
- **Earlier than a past point:** past perfect can make the order explicit; it is not compulsory in every two-event story.
- **State/action meaning:** *have a book* versus *have lunch*; *see* versus *meet*.
- **Future time clause:** *when he comes / after he has arrived*, normally not *will* for ordinary future time.
- **If he comes → will help; if he came → would help; if he had come → would have helped.**
- **It's time he went:** action needed now, despite the past form.
- **Tense consistency means a coherent timeline**, not identical tense in every clause.

> **⚠️ Do not memorise these as absolute rules**
>
> “Yesterday = only simple past”; “since/for = always continuous”; “all stative verbs can never be continuous”; “two past actions must use past perfect”; “by = always future perfect”; “after/when can only take simple present”; “I must take shall”; “all verbs in one sentence must use one tense”. Each shortcut can produce wrong answers.
